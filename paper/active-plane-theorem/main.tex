\begin{abstract}
Let a stable linear Ornstein--Uhlenbeck system have a symmetric drift whose restriction to a
two-dimensional invariant subspace has relaxation rates $D_1,D_2>0$. We start from an exact
identity for the stationary covariance $\Sig$ solving the Lyapunov equation, valid for an
\emph{arbitrary} symmetric forcing $Q$ on that subspace:
\[
  4D_1D_2\det\Sig \;=\; \det Q \;+\; \nu^2 Q_{12}^2,
  \qquad \nu=\frac{D_1-D_2}{D_1+D_2},
\]
where $Q_{12}$ is the off-diagonal entry in the drift eigenbasis. This is the $2\times2$
Hadamard-product determinant expansion behind Oppenheim's inequality, applied to the Cauchy
matrix $1/(D_i+D_j)$, and we claim nothing for it; it is recorded because it makes what follows
transparent. No rank-one, isotropy or positivity hypothesis is needed. Applied to the affine family
$Q(\tau)=q_bI+(\tau-1)H$ with $H$ symmetric of any rank, this gives
$4D_1D_2\det\Sig(\tau)=q_b^2+q_b(\tr H)(\tau-1)+K(\tau-1)^2$ with $K=\det H+\nu^2H_{12}^2$, and
hence our main structural result: for $q_b>0$ the determinant is a \emph{palindromic} quadratic
in $\tau$ \emph{if and only if the deformation is trace-normalized}, $\tr H=q_b$. Palindromicity
then forces the reciprocal coordinate $w=\tau+1/\tau$: the normalized determinant is exactly
affine in $w$, yielding an exact generalized-variance threshold. Rank-one heterogeneous
excitation $H=q_be e\T$ is the special case $\tr H=q_b$ holds automatically, and recovers
earlier two-node results with $\tau=r^2$. We separate the algebraic theorem from the
covariance-admissible family: $H$ positive semidefinite with $\tr H=q_b$ keeps $K\ge0$ and
$Q(\tau)\succ0$ for every $\tau>0$. Graph-Laplacian corollaries give exact optimal thresholds
$34$, $35+15\sqrt5$ and $247/2$ for $K_2$, $P_3$ and $K_3$. A three-mode excitation with three
distinct rates makes the determinant genuinely cubic, with leading coefficient proportional to
$\prod_{i<j}(D_i-D_j)^2$, so the quadratic closure is tied to having two distinct active
relaxation rates. The identity, the trace criterion, the reciprocal reduction and the
admissibility bounds are machine-checked in Lean~4 with mathlib.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Linear Ornstein--Uhlenbeck (OU) systems are among the few multivariate stochastic models whose
stationary statistics are available in closed form: for a Hurwitz drift the stationary
covariance solves an algebraic Lyapunov equation
\cite{UhlenbeckOrnstein1930,Gardiner2004,Vatiwutipong2019}. This makes them a natural setting in
which to ask how \emph{heterogeneous} forcing interacts with coupling, and the question has been
studied directly: Barucca analyses localization in covariance matrices of coupled heterogeneous
OU processes \cite{Barucca2014}; Ferreira, Metz and Barucca develop a random-matrix ensemble for
OU covariances with heterogeneous temperatures \cite{FerreiraMetzBarucca2025}; Godr\`eche and
Luck give exact nonequilibrium stationary states for linear Langevin systems at two temperatures
\cite{GodrecheLuck2019}; and the Brownian-gyrator literature studies the same two-temperature
structure in a planar model \cite{FilligerReimann2007,DotsenkoEtAl2013}.

A scalar summary of such a covariance is its determinant, Wilks's generalized variance
\cite{Wilks1932}, which is monotone in the differential entropy of the stationary Gaussian
\cite{CoverThomas2005}. Earlier work of the author \cite{DeJesus2026ou} analysed two diffusively
coupled OU processes with unequal noise amplitudes and found that this determinant can exceed
its uncoupled value at finite coupling if and only if the noise-amplitude ratio exceeds
$3+2\sqrt2$ --- an exact algebraic threshold in a two-node model.

The present paper grew out of trying to say exactly what makes that threshold work, and the
answer turned out to be neither the graph nor the geometry we first guessed at. An earlier
formulation of ours isolated a two-dimensional invariant subspace of the drift carrying a
\emph{rank-one} deformation of an isotropic forcing, and explained the resulting reciprocal
structure by a self-duality of that rank-one family. That explanation is correct but not sharp.

What is actually true is simpler and more general. For a symmetric drift restricted to a
two-dimensional invariant subspace, the stationary covariance obeys the exact identity
\begin{equation}
  4D_1D_2\det\Sig \;=\; \det Q + \nu^2Q_{12}^2,
  \qquad \nu=\frac{D_1-D_2}{D_1+D_2}, \label{eq:sharpintro}
\end{equation}
for \emph{any} symmetric forcing $Q$ --- no rank-one, no isotropy, no positivity. Feeding an
affine family $Q(\tau)=q_bI+(\tau-1)H$ into \eqref{eq:sharpintro} makes the determinant a
quadratic in $\tau$ whose leading and constant coefficients coincide precisely when
$\tr H=q_b$. So the mechanism is a \emph{trace normalization} of the deformation, and rank-one
excitation is simply the case in which that normalization is automatic, because a unit rank-one
$q_bee\T$ has trace $q_b$ whatever direction $e$ points. Isotropy of the baseline is not needed
either, and forcing-level self-duality --- which does hold in the rank-one isotropic model --- is
sufficient but not necessary: we exhibit an anisotropic baseline where self-duality fails and
palindromicity survives.

Only once palindromicity is established does the reciprocal coordinate appear. It is not posited
as a convenient substitution: a palindromic quadratic divided by $\tau$ is an affine function of
$w=\tau+1/\tau$, and that is what produces an exact threshold. The chain is
\[
\begin{gathered}
  \text{Lyapunov identity}\ \to\ \text{affine forcing family}\ \to\ \text{trace normalization}\\
  \to\ \text{palindromic determinant}\ \to\ w=\tau+1/\tau\ \to\ \text{exact threshold},
\end{gathered}
\]
and each arrow is proved before the next is used.

We are careful throughout to separate the algebra from the modelling. Identity
\eqref{eq:sharpintro} and the trace criterion hold for any symmetric $H$ and any real $\tau$;
but $Q(\tau)$ is a forcing covariance only where it is positive definite, and the threshold
statement needs $K\ge0$. Both are guaranteed by taking $H$ positive semidefinite with
$\tr H=q_b$, and we say so rather than quietly assuming it.

\paragraph{What is standard and what is not.} Almost every ingredient is classical, and we are
explicit about it: the OU stationary covariance and the Lyapunov equation \cite{Gardiner2004};
the modal solution $\Sig_{ij}=Q_{ij}/(D_i+D_j)$, standard in the heterogeneous-OU literature
\cite{Barucca2014,GodrecheLuck2019}, and its Cauchy--Hadamard form
\cite{PalittaLefteriu2022,YadykinEtAl2023}; the determinant identity \eqref{eq:sharpintro}
itself, which is the $2\times2$ Hadamard expansion behind Oppenheim's inequality
(Remark~\ref{rem:hadamard}); the reciprocal coordinate, an invertible function of the usual
two-temperature combination (Remark~\ref{rem:wknown}); the reduction of a self-reciprocal
polynomial through $t+1/t$ \cite{Vieira2019}; the trace/determinant shape statistic and its
relation to Mauchly's sphericity test \cite{Mauchly1940,Muirhead1982}; and the Newton-identity
obstruction in dimension $n\ge3$.

What we did not locate in the literature we searched is the \emph{trace criterion} of
Theorem~\ref{thm:trace}, any published statement that a covariance determinant is palindromic in
a forcing parameter, or the resulting exact generalized-variance threshold minimized over a
coupling strength. We state that conservatively in Section~\ref{sec:priorart} and claim nothing
stronger.

\paragraph{Scope.} All results are exact real algebra about a linear-Gaussian model. Nothing here
is asserted about nonlinear, biological, cognitive or social systems, and no physical universality
is attributed to any of the constants that appear.

\section{Setting and notation}\label{sec:setting}

Consider a linear stochastic differential equation on $\Real^n$,
\begin{equation}
  dX = MX\,dt + B\,dW, \qquad Q := BB\T, \label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}
with $M$ Hurwitz. The stationary covariance $\Sig$ is the unique solution of the algebraic
Lyapunov equation
\begin{equation}
  M\Sig + \Sig M\T = -Q. \label{eq:lyap}
\end{equation}
Existence and uniqueness of the stationary Gaussian law for \eqref{eq:sde} is classical
\cite{Gardiner2004,Vatiwutipong2019}; we take \eqref{eq:lyap} as the object of study and prove
statements about it.

\begin{definition}[Active plane and excitation direction]\label{def:active}
\textbf{We assume throughout that $M$ is symmetric}, so that it is orthogonally diagonalizable
and the modal basis below is orthonormal; this hypothesis is load-bearing and is not a
convenience. Everything in Sections~\ref{sec:thm1}--\ref{sec:threshold} rests on the modal
solution \eqref{eq:modal}, which is false for non-normal drift.

Let $\Pi\subset\Real^n$ be a two-dimensional subspace invariant under $M$, with eigenmodes
$u,v$ and relaxation rates $D_1,D_2>0$; that is, in the orthonormal basis $(u,v)$ of $\Pi$,
\[
  M|_\Pi = \operatorname{diag}(-D_1,-D_2).
\]
Let $e=cu+sv$ with $c^2+s^2=1$ be a unit vector in $\Pi$, the \emph{excitation direction}, and let
$\eperp=-su+cv$. We call $\Pi$ the \emph{active plane}. Throughout, $e$ denotes this direction and
never Euler's number.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Rank-one heterogeneous forcing]\label{def:forcing}
For a baseline intensity $q_b>0$ and a parameter $\tau>0$, the forcing on $\Pi$ is
\begin{equation}
  Q(\tau) \;=\; q_b\bigl(I + (\tau-1)\,ee\T\bigr), \label{eq:forcing}
\end{equation}
whose eigenvalues on $\Pi$ are $q_b\tau$ along $e$ and $q_b$ along $\eperp$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[$\tau$ is a variance ratio]\label{rem:variance}
This point is a frequent source of confusion and we fix it now. The parameter $\tau$ in
\eqref{eq:forcing} is a ratio of \emph{forcing eigenvalues}, i.e.\ of noise \emph{variances} (or
temperatures), not of noise \emph{amplitudes}. When the two-node model of \cite{DeJesus2026ou} is
recovered in Section~\ref{sec:K2}, its amplitude ratio $r$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \tau = r^2, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
  \tau+\frac1\tau = \Bigl(r+\frac1r\Bigr)^{\!2}-2. \label{eq:tvsr}
\end{equation}
So $w=\tau+1/\tau$ and $s(r)=r+1/r$ are \emph{different} reciprocal invariants of the same
physical situation, related by \eqref{eq:tvsr}; conflating them misreports a threshold by a
square. We always write $w$ for the variance invariant. This remark fixes notation only: the
coordinate $w$ plays no role until Section~\ref{sec:thm3}, where it is \emph{forced} by the
palindromic identity rather than chosen for convenience.
\end{remark}

We write $\nu$ for the rate contrast and $\Aco$ for the coefficient that will govern everything:
\begin{equation}
  \nu \;=\; \frac{D_1-D_2}{D_1+D_2}, \qquad
  \Aco \;=\; c^2s^2\,\nu^2. \label{eq:nuA}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:plane} shows the configuration.

\section{The general determinant identity}\label{sec:thm1}

\begin{theorem}[Stationary covariance on the plane]\label{thm:cov}
Let $D_1,D_2>0$ and let $Q=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}q_{11}&q_{12}\\ q_{12}&q_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$
be any symmetric forcing on $\Pi$. Then \eqref{eq:lyap} restricted to $\Pi$ has the unique
solution
\begin{equation}
  \Sig_{ij}=\frac{Q_{ij}}{D_i+D_j},
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
  \Sig=\begin{pmatrix}
    \dfrac{q_{11}}{2D_1} & \dfrac{q_{12}}{D_1+D_2}\\[2.2ex]
    \dfrac{q_{12}}{D_1+D_2} & \dfrac{q_{22}}{2D_2}\end{pmatrix}. \label{eq:modal}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In the eigenbasis \eqref{eq:lyap} reads entrywise $-(D_i+D_j)\Sig_{ij}=-Q_{ij}$, and
$D_i+D_j>0$.
\end{proof}

Equation \eqref{eq:modal} is the standard modal form of the Lyapunov solution for a symmetric
drift, used throughout the heterogeneous-OU literature
\cite{Barucca2014,GodrecheLuck2019,FerreiraMetzBarucca2025}; we claim no novelty for it. The
next identity is the one everything rests on.

\begin{theorem}[General two-mode determinant identity]\label{thm:sharp}
Let $D_1,D_2>0$ and let $Q$ be any symmetric matrix on $\Pi$. With $\Sig$ as in
\eqref{eq:modal},
\begin{equation}
  4D_1D_2\,\det\Sig \;=\; \det Q \;+\; \nu^2\,Q_{12}^2,
  \qquad \nu=\frac{D_1-D_2}{D_1+D_2}. \label{eq:sharp}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:modal},
$\det\Sig=\dfrac{q_{11}q_{22}}{4D_1D_2}-\dfrac{q_{12}^2}{(D_1+D_2)^2}$, so
\[
  4D_1D_2\det\Sig
  = q_{11}q_{22}-\frac{4D_1D_2}{(D_1+D_2)^2}\,q_{12}^2
  = q_{11}q_{22}-\bigl(1-\nu^2\bigr)q_{12}^2
  = \det Q+\nu^2q_{12}^2,
\]
using $(D_1+D_2)^2-(D_1-D_2)^2=4D_1D_2$, i.e. $4D_1D_2/(D_1+D_2)^2=1-\nu^2$.
\end{proof}

The content of \eqref{eq:sharp} is a mismatch of denominators. The diagonal entries of $\Sig$
carry $2D_i$ and the off-diagonal carries $D_1+D_2$; if the off-diagonal carried the geometric
mean $2\sqrt{D_1D_2}$ instead, the determinant would be exactly $\det Q/(4D_1D_2)$ and nothing
further would happen. The arithmetic mean is larger, the deficit is precisely $\nu^2$, and the
whole of this paper is what that deficit does.

\begin{remark}[\eqref{eq:sharp} is a known expansion, and we do not claim it]\label{rem:hadamard}
Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} should be read as bookkeeping, not as a discovery, and it is worth being
explicit about where it sits. Writing $C_{ij}=1/(D_i+D_j)$ for the Cauchy matrix of the rate
pairs, \eqref{eq:modal} says exactly
\begin{equation}
  \Sig = C\circ Q \label{eq:hadamard}
\end{equation}
with $\circ$ the Hadamard (entrywise) product --- a standard way of writing the solution of a
Sylvester or Lyapunov equation with diagonal coefficients
\cite{PalittaLefteriu2022,YadykinEtAl2023}. For $2\times2$ matrices the determinant of a
Hadamard product expands as
\begin{equation}
  \det(C\circ Q) = \det C\cdot q_{11}q_{22} + C_{12}^2\,\det Q, \label{eq:hadexp}
\end{equation}
and \eqref{eq:sharp} is \eqref{eq:hadexp} multiplied by $4D_1D_2$, using
$4D_1D_2\det C=\nu^2$ and $4D_1D_2C_{12}^2=1-\nu^2$. Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
  4D_1D_2\det\Sig = \nu^2\,q_{11}q_{22} + (1-\nu^2)\det Q, \label{eq:convex}
\end{equation}
a convex combination of $q_{11}q_{22}$ and $\det Q$ with weight $\nu^2$.

Equation \eqref{eq:hadexp} is the $2\times2$ case underlying Oppenheim's inequality
$\det(A\circ B)\ge\det A\prod_ib_{ii}$ for positive semidefinite $A,B$
\cite[Thm.~7.8.16, eq.~(7.8.17), p.~509]{HornJohnson2012}, originally due to
Oppenheim \cite{Oppenheim1930}.
Oppenheim gives the inequality; \eqref{eq:sharp} simply records the remainder, which at $n=2$ is
exactly $C_{12}^2\det Q$. The combination ``Cauchy--Hadamard solution, then Oppenheim, then a
determinant bound'' has been used before --- \cite[\S4.5]{SchlickDemissie2011} does precisely
this for a discrete-time steady-state covariance, stopping at the inequality. We therefore claim
nothing for Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} beyond convenience of form. What we do claim begins in
Section~\ref{sec:thm2}.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Correlation transfer]\label{cor:rho}
With $\rho_\Sig=\Sig_{12}/\sqrt{\Sig_{11}\Sig_{22}}$ and $\rho_Q=q_{12}/\sqrt{q_{11}q_{22}}$,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\Sig = \frac{2\sqrt{D_1D_2}}{D_1+D_2}\;\rho_Q = \sqrt{1-\nu^2}\;\rho_Q. \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
The stationary correlation is the forcing correlation damped by the ratio of the geometric to
the arithmetic mean of the relaxation rates.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Direct from \eqref{eq:modal}; and $\bigl(2\sqrt{D_1D_2}/(D_1+D_2)\bigr)^2=4D_1D_2/(D_1+D_2)^2=1-\nu^2$.
\end{proof}

Corollary~\ref{cor:rho} is an equivalent packaging of Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} and is likewise not
claimed as new; it is the kind of statement that may well sit unreferenced in the bivariate-OU or
correlation-transfer literature, and we have not exhausted that search.

\begin{remark}[What is \emph{not} assumed]
Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} assumes only that $Q$ is symmetric. It is not assumed rank one, not
isotropic, not positive semidefinite, and $\tau$ has not yet been introduced. The drift, by
contrast, \emph{is} assumed symmetric on $\Pi$ (Definition~\ref{def:active}); that hypothesis is
load-bearing, since \eqref{eq:modal} fails for non-normal drift.
\end{remark}

\section{Affine forcing and the trace criterion}\label{sec:thm2}

We now deform the forcing along a one-parameter family.

\begin{definition}[Affine forcing family]\label{def:affine}
For a baseline intensity $q_b>0$, a symmetric $H=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}h_1&h_{12}\\ h_{12}&h_2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$
and $\tau\in\Real$, set
\begin{equation}
  Q(\tau) \;=\; q_b I + (\tau-1)H. \label{eq:affine}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Affine determinant formula]\label{thm:affine}
With $\Sig(\tau)$ the stationary covariance of \eqref{eq:affine},
\begin{equation}
  4D_1D_2\det\Sig(\tau)
  \;=\; q_b^2 \;+\; q_b(\tr H)(\tau-1) \;+\; K(\tau-1)^2,
  \qquad K:=\det H+\nu^2H_{12}^2. \label{eq:affinedet}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$Q(\tau)$ has off-diagonal $(\tau-1)h_{12}$ and
$\det Q(\tau)=q_b^2+q_b(\tr H)(\tau-1)+(\det H)(\tau-1)^2$, the standard expansion of the
determinant of $q_bI+(\tau-1)H$ in dimension two. Substituting both into \eqref{eq:sharp} and
collecting the $(\tau-1)^2$ terms gives \eqref{eq:affinedet}.
\end{proof}

Expanding \eqref{eq:affinedet} in powers of $\tau$,
\begin{equation}
  4D_1D_2\det\Sig(\tau)
  = \underbrace{K}_{\text{leading}}\tau^2
  + \bigl(q_b\tr H-2K\bigr)\tau
  + \underbrace{\bigl(q_b^2-q_b\tr H+K\bigr)}_{\text{constant}}. \label{eq:expand}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Trace criterion for palindromicity]\label{thm:trace}
The leading and constant coefficients in \eqref{eq:expand} are equal if and only if
$q_b(\tr H-q_b)=0$. In particular, for $q_b>0$,
\begin{equation}
  4D_1D_2\det\Sig(\tau)\ \text{is palindromic in }\tau
  \iff \tr H=q_b. \label{eq:tracecrit}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Leading minus constant equals $K-(q_b^2-q_b\tr H+K)=q_b\tr H-q_b^2=q_b(\tr H-q_b)$. For
$q_b>0$ this vanishes iff $\tr H=q_b$.
\end{proof}

This is the structural statement of the paper, and it is worth saying plainly what it replaces.
The reciprocal structure is \emph{not} caused by the deformation being rank one, nor by the
baseline being isotropic. It is caused by the deformation carrying exactly the baseline's share
of trace. Rank one is the case where that happens for free.

\begin{remark}[Why the criterion is a trace, and why it is a $2\times2$ statement]\label{rem:whytrace}
Three features of Theorem~\ref{thm:trace} are worth isolating.

\emph{(i) Where the coefficients come from.} Writing $\Sig(\tau)=S_0+\tau S_1$ with
$S_1=C\circ H$ and $S_0=C\circ(q_bI-H)$, the leading and constant coefficients of
$\det\Sig(\tau)$ are exactly $\det(C\circ H)$ and $\det\bigl(C\circ(q_bI-H)\bigr)$. So
palindromicity is the single scalar equation
$\det(C\circ H)=\det\bigl(C\circ(q_bI-H)\bigr)$.

\emph{(ii) Why the drift drops out.} In that equation the off-diagonal of $H$ enters only through
$h_{12}^2$, which is invariant under $H\mapsto q_bI-H$ (it sends $h_{12}\mapsto-h_{12}$). Every
trace of the Cauchy structure --- and with it $\nu$, and the whole content of
Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} --- therefore cancels, leaving only the diagonal part
$q_b^2-q_b(h_1+h_2)$. This is why the criterion $\tr H=q_b$ contains no $D_1,D_2$: it is
\emph{independent of the drift}, which is not obvious from the statement.

\emph{(iii) Why it is special to two modes.} For $n$ modes, self-reciprocity of a degree-$n$
determinant requires $n-1$ coefficient matchings, not one. Already at $n=3$ two conditions
appear and $\tr H=q_b$ is only one of them. The clean iff is a two-mode statement, and
Section~\ref{sec:threemode} is where that boundary is made quantitative.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A degenerate case the criterion does not see]\label{rem:degenpoly}
``Palindromic'' in Theorem~\ref{thm:trace} means \emph{leading coefficient equals constant
coefficient}, which is the form used throughout. When $K=0$ both are zero and the criterion is
satisfied vacuously by a polynomial that is only of degree one: for instance
$H=\operatorname{diag}(q_b,0)$ has $\tr H=q_b$ and gives
$4D_1D_2\det\Sig(\tau)=q_b^2\tau$. That is the case of an excitation aligned with a single drift
eigenmode, i.e.\ $\Aco=0$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:degenerate}, and it carries no threshold
(Remark~\ref{rem:edge}). A reader who prefers to define palindromicity through the actual degree
should read Theorem~\ref{thm:trace} together with $K\neq0$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Trace-normalized determinant]\label{thm:tracenorm}
If $\tr H=q_b$ then
\begin{equation}
  4D_1D_2\det\Sig(\tau) \;=\; K(\tau-1)^2+q_b^2\,\tau
  \;=\; K\tau^2+(q_b^2-2K)\tau+K. \label{eq:tracenorm}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $\tr H=q_b$ in \eqref{eq:affinedet}: the linear term becomes $q_b^2(\tau-1)$, and
$q_b^2+q_b^2(\tau-1)=q_b^2\tau$.
\end{proof}

\section{The reciprocal reduction}\label{sec:thm3}

Only now, with palindromicity in hand, does the reciprocal coordinate enter. A palindromic
polynomial of even degree $2m$ in $t$ is a degree-$m$ polynomial in $t+1/t$ after division by
$t^m$; the general statement is classical (see \cite[\S2]{Vieira2019}), and we use only $m=1$,
proved in line.

\begin{definition}
For $\tau>0$ set $w=\tau+\dfrac1\tau$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:wge2}
For $\tau>0$, $w-2=(\tau-1)^2/\tau\ge0$, with equality iff $\tau=1$. Hence $w\ge2$ always.
\end{lemma}

\begin{remark}[$w$ is not a new variable]\label{rem:wknown}
If $\tau$ is read as a ratio of temperatures $T_1/T_2$, then
$w-2=(T_1-T_2)^2/(T_1T_2)$, a combination that appears throughout the two-temperature linear
literature \cite{FilligerReimann2007,DotsenkoEtAl2013,GodrecheLuck2019}. So $w$ is an invertible
function of a standard quantity and we claim nothing for the coordinate itself. What the results
below assert is that the normalized determinant is \emph{exactly affine} in it, and precisely
when.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Reciprocal reduction]\label{cor:reciprocal}
If $\tr H=q_b$ and $\tau>0$, then
\begin{equation}
  \frac{4D_1D_2\det\Sig(\tau)}{\tau} \;=\; q_b^2+K\,(w-2),
  \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
  \frac{4D_1D_2\det\Sig(\tau)}{q_b^2\,\tau} \;=\; 1+\frac{K}{q_b^2}(w-2). \label{eq:reciprocal}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Divide \eqref{eq:tracenorm} by $\tau$ and use Lemma~\ref{lem:wge2}:
$K(\tau-1)^2/\tau+q_b^2=K(w-2)+q_b^2$.
\end{proof}

Figure~\ref{fig:affine} plots \eqref{eq:reciprocal}. The dependence on the heterogeneity is
exactly affine in $w$, with slope $K/q_b^2$ and value $1$ at $w=2$.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{figures/fig2.pdf}
\caption{The normalized determinant \eqref{eq:reciprocal} as a function of the reciprocal
coordinate $w$, for several values of the slope. The dependence is exactly affine, with value

\section{Admissibility: algebra versus covariance}\label{sec:admissible}

Everything above is an identity between polynomials and holds for every symmetric $H$ and every
real $\tau$. Two further things are needed before it describes a stochastic system, and we state
them separately rather than folding them into the hypotheses.

\begin{proposition}[Sign of $K$]\label{prop:Ksign}
$K=\det H+\nu^2H_{12}^2=h_1h_2-(1-\nu^2)h_{12}^2$. If $H$ is positive semidefinite then
$K\ge0$. If $H$ is indefinite, $K$ may be negative: $H=\operatorname{diag}(2,-1)$ has
$\tr H=1$ and $K=-2$. Moreover $K=0$ is attained, e.g.\ $H=\operatorname{diag}(q_b,0)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$0\le1-\nu^2\le1$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:mixing} below, so $K\ge\det H$; if $H$ is positive
semidefinite then $\det H\ge0$ and $\nu^2H_{12}^2\ge0$. The two examples are direct.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Positivity of the forcing]\label{prop:posdef}
Let $H$ be positive semidefinite with $\tr H=q_b>0$. Then $Q(\tau)\succ0$ for every $\tau>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$H$ has eigenvalues $\eta$ and $q_b-\eta$ with $0\le\eta\le q_b$. The eigenvalues of
$Q(\tau)=q_bI+(\tau-1)H$ are $q_b+(\tau-1)\eta_i$, which for $\tau>0$ are bounded below by
$\min\{q_b,\ \tau q_b\}>0$. Equivalently, by \eqref{eq:affinedet} with $\tr H=q_b$,
$\det Q(\tau)=q_b^2\tau+(\tau-1)^2\det H>0$ and $\tr Q(\tau)=q_b(1+\tau)>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Admissible family]\label{def:admissible}
We call \eqref{eq:affine} \emph{admissible} when $q_b>0$ and $H$ is positive semidefinite with
$\tr H=q_b$. By Propositions~\ref{prop:Ksign} and \ref{prop:posdef} an admissible family has
$K\ge0$ and $Q(\tau)\succ0$ for all $\tau>0$, so \eqref{eq:reciprocal} is a statement about
genuine forcing covariances and its slope is nonnegative.
\end{definition}

\section{Rank-one excitation as a special case}\label{sec:rankone}

\begin{corollary}[Rank-one heterogeneous excitation]\label{cor:rankone}
Let $e=cu+sv$ with $c^2+s^2=1$ and put $H=q_b\,ee\T$, i.e.\
$Q(\tau)=q_b\bigl(I+(\tau-1)ee\T\bigr)$. Then $\tr H=q_b$ automatically, $H$ is positive
semidefinite, $\det H=0$, $H_{12}=q_bcs$, and
\begin{equation}
  K=q_b^2\,c^2s^2\nu^2,
  \qquad
  \frac{4D_1D_2\det\Sig(\tau)}{q_b^2\tau}=1+\Aco\,(w-2),
  \quad \Aco:=c^2s^2\nu^2. \label{eq:rankone}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$\tr(q_bee\T)=q_b\|e\|^2=q_b$ and $\det(q_bee\T)=0$ since $ee\T$ has rank one. Then
$K=0+\nu^2(q_bcs)^2$. Divide by $q_b^2$ in \eqref{eq:reciprocal}.
\end{proof}

This is the theorem of our earlier formulation, now with its hypotheses explained: the rank-one
model works because a unit rank-one deformation is trace-normalized \emph{for every direction
$e$}, and it is admissible because such an $H$ is positive semidefinite. The direction enters
only through $c^2s^2$.

\begin{proposition}[Degeneracy and size, rank-one case]\label{prop:degenerate}
For the rank-one family, $\Aco\ge0$ and
\begin{equation}
  \Aco=0 \iff c=0\ \text{ or }\ s=0\ \text{ or }\ D_1=D_2, \label{eq:degen}
\end{equation}
while the reciprocal contribution $\Aco(w-2)$ vanishes iff $\Aco=0$ or $\tau=1$.
\end{proposition}

The readings are: $c=0$ or $s=0$, the excitation lies in a single drift eigenmode, so forcing
and drift are simultaneously diagonal and the covariance cannot mix them; $D_1=D_2$, no
relaxation contrast; $\tau=1$, no heterogeneity. A nonzero reciprocal contribution needs
\emph{both} anisotropies, in the drift and in the forcing.

\begin{remark}
Equation~\eqref{eq:degen} is specific to rank one. For general trace-normalized $H$ the
coefficient is $K$, and $K$ can be nonzero with $\nu=0$: the isotropic scaling
$H=\tfrac{q_b}{2}I$ gives $K=q_b^2/4$ for any rates. So the degeneracy analysis must be read as
a statement about the rank-one family, not about \eqref{eq:tracecrit}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Modal-mixture bound]\label{prop:mixing}
If $c^2+s^2=1$ then $c^2s^2\le\tfrac14$, with equality iff $c^2=s^2=\tfrac12$. If $D_1,D_2>0$
then $\nu^2<1$. Hence $0\le\Aco<\tfrac14$ in the rank-one case.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\tfrac14-c^2s^2=(c^2-\tfrac12)^2\ge0$; and $(D_1+D_2)^2-(D_1-D_2)^2=4D_1D_2>0$.
\end{proof}

\section{Self-duality: sufficient, not necessary}\label{sec:selfdual}

The rank-one isotropic model has an extra symmetry that makes the reciprocal structure visually
obvious, and it is worth recording --- together with the fact that it is not the cause.

\begin{proposition}[Self-duality of the rank-one isotropic family]\label{prop:selfdual}
Let $c^2+s^2=1$, $\eperp=-su+cv$, $\tau>0$, and $Q_e(\tau)=q_b(I+(\tau-1)ee\T)$. Then
\begin{equation}
  Q_e(\tau)=\tau\,Q_{\eperp}\!\left(\tfrac1\tau\right). \label{eq:selfdual}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$ee\T+\eperp\eperpT=I$, so $Q_e(\tau)=q_b(\tau\,ee\T+\eperp\eperpT)$; and
$\tau q_b\bigl(I+(\tau^{-1}-1)\eperp\eperpT\bigr)=q_b(\tau\,ee\T+\eperp\eperpT)$ likewise.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The counterexample]\label{rem:selfdualnotnec}
Self-duality \eqref{eq:selfdual} is \emph{not} necessary for palindromicity. Take an anisotropic
baseline diagonal in the drift eigenbasis, $Q_0=\operatorname{diag}(p,q)$ with $p\neq q$, and
deform along $e$ with the scale $\mu=1/(e\T Q_0^{-1}e)$, i.e.\
$Q(\tau)=Q_0+\mu(\tau-1)ee\T$. Then:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item $\det Q(\tau)=\tau\det Q_0$ exactly --- that normalization is precisely what makes $\tau$
  the \emph{determinant ratio} of the forcing;
\item consequently $\det Q(\tau)$ is linear in $\tau$ while $Q_{12}(\tau)=\mu(\tau-1)cs$, so by
  \eqref{eq:sharp} the determinant is $\tau\det Q_0+\nu^2\mu^2c^2s^2(\tau-1)^2$, manifestly
  palindromic;
\item but \eqref{eq:selfdual} fails for $p\neq q$.
\end{itemize}
So palindromicity survives where forcing-level self-duality does not. We therefore present
\eqref{eq:selfdual} as one transparent mechanism in a special model, not as the reason for the
theorem. The reason is Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} together with $\tr H=q_b$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Non-commuting baselines]\label{rem:noncommuting}
For a baseline with a nonzero off-diagonal in the drift eigenbasis, the analogous condition
still has solutions, but the required scale then depends on the drift as well as on the
forcing, which we do not regard as a natural parameterization. We record only that commutation
of the baseline with the drift is sufficient, not necessary, and do not pursue the exceptional
family here.
\end{remark}

\section{The threshold theorem}\label{sec:threshold}

\begin{theorem}[Exact threshold]\label{thm:threshold}
Let $\Phi>0$ and $C>0$ and put $V=\Phi\bigl(1+C(w-2)\bigr)$. Then
\begin{equation}
  V>1 \iff w>w^{*}, \qquad w^{*}=2+\frac{1-\Phi}{\Phi C}. \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$w-\bigl(2+\tfrac{1-\Phi}{\Phi C}\bigr)=\bigl(\Phi(1+C(w-2))-1\bigr)/(\Phi C)$ identically, and
$\Phi C>0$.
\end{proof}

In the applications $C=K/q_b^2$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:reciprocal}, and $C=\Aco$ in the rank-one
case.

\begin{remark}[Edge cases, stated rather than hidden]\label{rem:edge}
Since $w\ge2$ always (Lemma~\ref{lem:wge2}), three regimes must be distinguished.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item $\Phi<1$: then $w^{*}>2$ and \eqref{eq:threshold} is a genuine \emph{heterogeneity
  threshold}. This is the case in all graph applications below.
\item $\Phi=1$: $w^{*}=2$, so $V>1$ for every $\tau\neq1$. Any heterogeneity at all suffices.
\item $\Phi>1$: $w^{*}<2$ is unreachable and $V>1$ for every admissible $w$ ---
  \emph{automatic enhancement}, with no threshold.
\item $C=0$: excluded by the hypothesis $C>0$. Then $V=\Phi$ identically and no threshold
  exists; this is the degenerate case of Proposition~\ref{prop:degenerate}, and also the case
  $K=0$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:Ksign}.
\end{enumerate}
Finally, $w^{*}$ is a threshold in the \emph{variance} invariant; converting to an amplitude
ratio requires \eqref{eq:tvsr}.
\end{remark}

\section{Passive modes and the ambient system}\label{sec:passive}

Theorem~\ref{thm:trace} concerns a $2\times2$ block. We now record, as an analytic
statement, how that block sits inside an $n$-dimensional system --- and we are careful about what
is proved versus what is formalized.

\begin{proposition}[Determinant factorization]\label{prop:factor}
Let $M$ be symmetric and Hurwitz on $\Real^n$ with eigenmodes $\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n$ and rates
$D_1,\dots,D_n>0$, so that $\Pi=\operatorname{span}(\psi_1,\psi_2)$ and its orthogonal
complement are both $M$-invariant. Suppose the forcing is
\begin{equation}
  Q = q_b\bigl(I+(\tau-1)ee\T\bigr) \label{eq:Qambient}
\end{equation}
with $e$ a unit vector whose modal support lies in $\Pi=\operatorname{span}(\psi_1,\psi_2)$, i.e.
$e=c\psi_1+s\psi_2$ with $c^2+s^2=1$. Then $\Sig$ block-diagonalizes as
$\Sig|_\Pi\oplus\Sig|_{\Pi^{\perp}}$ and
\begin{equation}
  \det\Sig \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{q_b^2\,\tau\,\bigl[1+\Aco(w-2)\bigr]}{4D_1D_2}}_{\text{active block}}
  \;\cdot\;
  \prod_{j\ge3}\frac{q_b}{2D_j}
  \;=\; \frac{q_b^{\,n}\,\tau\,\bigl[1+\Aco(w-2)\bigr]}{2^{\,n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}D_j}. \label{eq:factored}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the modal basis $Q$ has entries $\widetilde Q_{ij}=q_b\delta_{ij}+q_b(\tau-1)\tilde e_i\tilde e_j$
with $\tilde e=(c,s,0,\dots,0)$. Hence $\widetilde Q_{ij}=0$ whenever $i\le2<j$ or $j\le2<i$, so
$\widetilde Q$ is block diagonal. By \eqref{eq:modal}, $\widetilde\Sig_{ij}=\widetilde Q_{ij}/(D_i+D_j)$
inherits the same block structure, and the determinant factors. The active factor is
Theorem~\ref{cor:reciprocal}; each passive mode contributes $\widetilde\Sig_{jj}=q_b/(2D_j)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The hypotheses are all needed]\label{rem:factorhyp}
Four separate conditions are doing work here, and forcing support alone is not enough:
(i) $M$ symmetric, so an orthonormal modal basis exists and \eqref{eq:modal} applies;
(ii) $\Pi$ invariant under $M$; (iii) $\Pi^{\perp}$ invariant, automatic for symmetric $M$;
(iv) the \emph{baseline} block-diagonal with respect to $\Pi\oplus\Pi^{\perp}$ --- automatic
here because the baseline is the isotropic $q_bI$, but false in general; and (v) the
deformation supported in $\Pi$. If the baseline couples the two blocks, or if the excitation
leaks into a passive mode, $\Sig$ does not block-diagonalize and the determinant does not
factor.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Formalized versus documented]\label{rem:formalized}
Proposition~\ref{prop:factor} is proved here analytically. The machine-checked theorem
(Section~\ref{sec:lean}) is the \emph{explicit active block} --- Theorems~\ref{thm:cov},
\ref{thm:trace}, \ref{cor:reciprocal} and \ref{thm:threshold}. The general
invariant-subspace reduction of Proposition~\ref{prop:factor} is documented analytically and is
\emph{not} formalized. We are explicit about this because the distinction is easy to blur: no
claim is made that Lean verifies an arbitrary $n\times n$ invariant-subspace reduction.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Three dimensions that must not be conflated}\label{sec:effdim}

Proposition~\ref{prop:factor} invites a piece of vocabulary, and the audit of this paper showed
that a single loose phrase will not do. Three different integers are in play, and the theorems
depend on the third:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\emph{ambient dimension} & $n$, the size of the system\\
\emph{modal support dimension} & the number of eigenmodes on which the deformation acts\\
\emph{distinct-rate dimension} & the number of \emph{distinct} relaxation rates among those modes\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The hypothesis of Theorems~\ref{thm:sharp}--\ref{thm:trace} is that the \textbf{distinct-rate
dimension is two}. The ambient dimension is irrelevant; the stationary law has full support on
$\Real^n$ and every mode is excited, the passive ones at the baseline intensity. The modal
support dimension may exceed two provided the extra modes share a rate --- which is exactly what
happens for $K_3$ in Section~\ref{sec:K3}, and is why that example is not a counterexample.

We therefore say the configuration has \emph{distinct-rate dimension two} and avoid saying that
the system ``is really two-dimensional''. This is interpretation attached to a hypothesis, not a
separate theorem, but it is the interpretation that makes the failure boundary in
Section~\ref{sec:threemode} sayable.

\section{The failure boundary: three distinct rates}\label{sec:threemode}

The quadratic closure is exactly a two-distinct-rate phenomenon, and we can say so with a
formula rather than a dimension count.

\begin{proposition}[Cubic response]\label{prop:cubic}
Let $M$ be symmetric with rates $D_1,D_2,D_3>0$ on a three-dimensional invariant subspace, and
let $Q(\tau)=q_b\bigl(I+(\tau-1)ee\T\bigr)$ with $e=\sum_ic_i\psi_i$, $\sum_ic_i^2=1$. Then
$\det\Sig(\tau)$ is a cubic in $\tau$ whose leading coefficient is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{q_b^3\,c_1^2c_2^2c_3^2\,(D_1-D_2)^2(D_1-D_3)^2(D_2-D_3)^2}
       {\prod_{i<j}(D_i+D_j)^2}\;\Big/\;\prod_i 2D_i, \label{eq:cubic}
\end{equation}
up to the normalization $\prod_i2D_i$ used to clear denominators. It vanishes if and only if
some $c_i=0$ --- the support drops to two modes --- or some $D_i=D_j$.
\end{proposition}

Two consequences. First, \eqref{eq:cubic} is a clean generalization of $\Aco=c^2s^2\nu^2$: the
same product structure, one factor of $(D_i-D_j)^2$ and one of $c_i^2$ per mode. Second, the
correct statement of the failure boundary is \emph{generic}, not absolute:

\begin{quote}
A genuinely three-mode excitation with three \emph{distinct} relaxation rates generically
produces a cubic response, and no single reciprocal coordinate can absorb it. If a rate is
repeated, or a modal weight vanishes, the cubic term disappears and the quadratic closure
returns.
\end{quote}

We have not investigated whether the cubic can be palindromic on some measure-zero set, and we
do not claim it cannot.

\section{Graph corollaries}\label{sec:graphs}

\begin{remark}[Weak coupling]\label{rem:weak}
Before specializing, one general fact puts the minimization below in context. If
$\Sig(\kappa)$ solves $(aI+\kappa S)\Sig+\Sig(aI+\kappa S)=Q$ for symmetric $S$ and fixed
positive-definite $Q$, then
$\tfrac{d}{d\kappa}\log\det\Sig|_{\kappa=0}=-\tr S/a$, independently of $Q$: differentiating
gives $\Sig'=-(S\Sig_0+\Sig_0S)/(2a)$ with $\Sig_0=Q/(2a)$, whence
$\tr(\Sig_0^{-1}\Sig')=-\tr S/a$. For a graph Laplacian $\tr L=2|E|$, so the initial rate is
$-2|E|/a<0$: infinitesimal coupling always contracts the generalized variance, and any
enhancement is necessarily a finite-coupling phenomenon. This generalizes the two-node statement
$A'(0)<0$ of \cite{DeJesus2026ou}.
\end{remark}



We now specialize to a concrete family. Let $G$ be a connected graph on $n$ nodes with Laplacian
$L$, and take
\begin{equation}
  M = -(aI+\kappa L), \qquad a>0,\ \kappa\ge0, \label{eq:graphdrift}
\end{equation}
so the modal rates are $D_j=a+\kappa\lambda_j$ for Laplacian eigenvalues
$0=\lambda_0\le\lambda_1\le\dots\le\lambda_{n-1}$. Let the forcing be \eqref{eq:Qambient} with $e$
a node indicator: heterogeneity at a single node. Define
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\kappa) = \prod_{j\ge1}\frac{a}{a+\kappa\lambda_j},
  \qquad
  V(\kappa,\tau) = \frac{\det\Sig(\kappa,\tau)}{\det\Sig(0,\tau)}. \label{eq:Phidef}
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[Graph form of the threshold]\label{cor:graph}
Suppose the node indicator $e$ has modal support in exactly two eigenmodes, with rates $D_1,D_2$
and weights $c^2,s^2$. Then
\begin{equation}
  V(\kappa,\tau) = \Phi(\kappa)\bigl[1+\Aco(\kappa)\,(w-2)\bigr],
  \qquad \Aco(\kappa)=c^2s^2\nu(\kappa)^2, \label{eq:graphV}
\end{equation}
and $V>1$ exactly when $w>w^{*}(\kappa)=2+(1-\Phi)/(\Phi\Aco)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:factored}, $\det\Sig(\kappa,\tau)=q_b^n\tau[1+\Aco(w-2)]/(2^n\prod_jD_j)$. At
$\kappa=0$ all rates equal $a$, so $\nu=0$, $\Aco=0$, and
$\det\Sig(0,\tau)=q_b^n\tau/(2^na^n)$. Dividing gives \eqref{eq:graphV} with
$\prod_j a/D_j=\Phi$. Theorem~\ref{thm:threshold} then applies.
\end{proof}

The optimal threshold for a given graph and forced node is
$w^{*}_{\min}=\min_{\kappa>0}w^{*}(\kappa)$: the least heterogeneity that can ever produce
enhancement, optimized over coupling strength. Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows the three curves
computed below.

\subsection{$K_2$: recovering the two-node model}\label{sec:K2}

For $K_2$ with $a=1$, the Laplacian eigenvalues are $0$ and $2$, so $D_1=1$, $D_2=1+2\kappa$, and
$\Phi=1/(1+2\kappa)$. A node indicator has weights $c^2=s^2=\tfrac12$ on the two modes, so
$c^2s^2=\tfrac14$ and
\[
  \Aco(\kappa)=\tfrac14\nu^2,\qquad \nu=\frac{1-(1+2\kappa)}{1+(1+2\kappa)}=\frac{-\kappa}{1+\kappa}.
\]
Corollary~\ref{cor:graph} gives
\begin{equation}
  V(\kappa,\tau)=\frac{1}{1+2\kappa}\left[1+\frac14\frac{\kappa^2}{(1+\kappa)^2}(w-2)\right],
  \label{eq:K2V}
\end{equation}
which is exactly the volume ratio of \cite{DeJesus2026ou} after the substitution $\tau=r^2$.
Minimizing $w^{*}(\kappa)$ over $\kappa>0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \kappa^{*}=1,\qquad w^{*}_{\min}=34. \label{eq:K2min}
\end{equation}
By \eqref{eq:tvsr}, $w=34$ corresponds to $r+1/r=6$, i.e.\ $r=3+2\sqrt2$, which is exactly the
threshold of \cite{DeJesus2026ou}. The coupling $\kappa^{*}=1$ is the tangency at which the
enhancement window of that paper closes to a point.

\begin{remark}
This is the sense in which the two-node result is a corollary rather than a separate theorem. It
is also a check on Remark~\ref{rem:variance}: the constant $34$ is a value of $w=\tau+1/\tau$,
while $6$ is a value of $r+1/r$, and $34=6^2-2$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{$P_3$: the path on three nodes}\label{sec:P3}

For $P_3$ the Laplacian has spectrum $\{0,1,3\}$ with orthonormal eigenvectors
\[
  \psi_0=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,1,1),\quad
  \psi_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,0,-1),\quad
  \psi_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1,-2,1).
\]
Force the \emph{centre} node, $e=(0,1,0)$. Its modal components are
$\langle\psi_0,e\rangle=1/\sqrt3$, $\langle\psi_1,e\rangle=0$, $\langle\psi_2,e\rangle=-2/\sqrt6$.
The vanishing middle component is the crucial structural fact: \emph{the centre-node excitation
has no support on the $\lambda=1$ mode}, so its active support is exactly two-dimensional and
Proposition~\ref{prop:factor} applies with
\[
  c^2=\tfrac13\ (\lambda=0),\qquad s^2=\tfrac23\ (\lambda=3),\qquad
  D_1=1,\quad D_2=1+3\kappa,
\]
the $\lambda=1$ mode being passive. Then $\nu=-3\kappa/(2+3\kappa)$ and
\[
  \Aco(\kappa)=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\frac{9\kappa^2}{(2+3\kappa)^2}
  =\frac{2\kappa^2}{(2+3\kappa)^2},
  \qquad
  \Phi(\kappa)=\frac{1}{(1+\kappa)(1+3\kappa)}.
\]
Substituting into $w^{*}=2+(1-\Phi)/(\Phi\Aco)$ and simplifying gives the exact curve
\begin{equation}
  w^{*}_{P_3}(\kappa)=\frac{27\kappa^3+72\kappa^2+64\kappa+16}{2\kappa}. \label{eq:P3curve}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}[The two-mode hypothesis is not vacuous: $P_3$ forced at an end]\label{rem:P3end}
It matters that the centre node was chosen. Forcing an \emph{end} node of $P_3$,
$e=(1,0,0)$, gives modal components $\bigl(1/\sqrt3,\,-1/\sqrt2,\,1/\sqrt6\bigr)$ --- all three
nonzero --- so the modal support dimension is three and the three rates
$1,\,1+\kappa,\,1+3\kappa$ are distinct. By Proposition~\ref{prop:cubic} the determinant is then
a genuine cubic in $\tau$, with leading coefficient
$\kappa^6/\bigl[4(\kappa+2)^2(2\kappa+1)^2(3\kappa+2)^2\bigr]$, and it is \emph{not} palindromic.
The affine form \eqref{eq:graphV} fails outright for that configuration. So the hypothesis of
Corollary~\ref{cor:graph} is a real restriction, and the simplest asymmetric case already
violates it.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[$P_3$ constant]\label{cor:P3}
$\displaystyle\min_{\kappa>0}w^{*}_{P_3}(\kappa)=35+15\sqrt5$, attained at
$\kappa^{*}=\dfrac{\sqrt5-1}{3}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The exact factorization
\[
  27\kappa^3+72\kappa^2+64\kappa+16-\bigl(35+15\sqrt5\bigr)\cdot2\kappa
  = 9\Bigl(\kappa-\tfrac{\sqrt5-1}{3}\Bigr)^{2}\bigl(3\kappa+6+2\sqrt5\bigr)
\]
holds identically. For $\kappa>0$ the second factor is positive, so the left side is
nonnegative, giving $w^{*}_{P_3}(\kappa)\ge35+15\sqrt5$; the double root shows equality at
$\kappa^{*}=(\sqrt5-1)/3$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{$K_3$: the triangle}\label{sec:K3}

For $K_3$ the spectrum is $\{0,3,3\}$. A node indicator has $c^2=1/3$ on $\psi_0$ and the
remaining weight $2/3$ inside the \emph{degenerate} $\lambda=3$ eigenspace. Because that
eigenspace is two-dimensional with a single common rate, one may rotate within it so that one
basis vector carries the entire projection; the excitation then has support in exactly two modes
and Proposition~\ref{prop:factor} applies with $c^2=1/3$, $s^2=2/3$, $D_1=1$, $D_2=1+3\kappa$.
The third mode is passive with the same rate $1+3\kappa$. Hence $\Aco$ is as for $P_3$ while
$\Phi$ differs:
\[
  \Aco(\kappa)=\frac{2\kappa^2}{(2+3\kappa)^2},
  \qquad
  \Phi(\kappa)=\frac{1}{(1+3\kappa)^2},
\]
giving the exact curve
\begin{equation}
  w^{*}_{K_3}(\kappa)=\frac{81\kappa^3+162\kappa^2+112\kappa+24}{2\kappa}. \label{eq:K3curve}
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[$K_3$ constant]\label{cor:K3}
$\displaystyle\min_{\kappa>0}w^{*}_{K_3}(\kappa)=\frac{247}{2}$, attained at
$\kappa^{*}=\dfrac13$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$81\kappa^3+162\kappa^2+112\kappa+24-\tfrac{247}{2}\cdot2\kappa
 =81\kappa^3+162\kappa^2-135\kappa+24=3(3\kappa-1)^2(3\kappa+8)$, which is nonnegative for
$\kappa>0$ and vanishes at $\kappa=1/3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why $K_3$ is not a counterexample]\label{rem:K3degen}
$K_3$ is the instructive case for Section~\ref{sec:effdim}. A node indicator in $K_3$ has
\emph{modal support dimension three}: it meets $\psi_0$ and both vectors of the $\lambda=3$
eigenspace. Nevertheless its \emph{distinct-rate dimension is two}, because that eigenspace is
degenerate --- both of its modes relax at $a+3\kappa$. Within a degenerate eigenspace any
orthonormal basis is admissible, so one may rotate to align a single basis vector with the
projection, after which the hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{prop:factor} holds with $c^2=1/3$,
$s^2=2/3$. Consistently with Proposition~\ref{prop:cubic}, the cubic coefficient carries a
factor $(D_i-D_j)^2$ that vanishes for the repeated rate, so the response is quadratic and
palindromic after all.

Where the relevant rates are \emph{distinct}, as for $P_3$, no such freedom exists and the
vanishing of a modal component is a genuine constraint on the graph and the forced node. This is
precisely the distinction that Section~\ref{sec:effdim} is designed to keep visible.
\end{remark}

\section{Two-dimensional spectral closure}\label{sec:closure}

A separate two-dimensional phenomenon explains why an earlier spectral argument closed with a
single scalar. For a positive-definite $2\times2$ matrix with eigenvalues $\mu_1\ge\mu_2>0$,
write $q=\mu_1/\mu_2\ge1$ and
\begin{equation}
  J_2(\Sig)=\frac{(\tr\Sig)^2}{\det\Sig}. \label{eq:J2}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:J2}
$J_2=q+\dfrac1q+2$, and $q\mapsto q+1/q$ is strictly increasing on $[1,\infty)$. Hence $J_2$
determines $q$ uniquely.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$(\mu_1+\mu_2)^2/(\mu_1\mu_2)=\mu_1/\mu_2+\mu_2/\mu_1+2$. Strict monotonicity of $q+1/q$ on
$[1,\infty)$ is elementary.
\end{proof}

This is standard. Scale-free trace/determinant functionals of a covariance matrix are the
classical sphericity diagnostics, going back to Mauchly \cite{Mauchly1940} and treated
textbook-style by Muirhead \cite[Ch.~8]{Muirhead1982}; trace/determinant eigenvalue bounds of this
type are likewise classical \cite{WolkowiczStyan1980,HornJohnson2012}. We claim no novelty. It is recorded because
it is the reason a single scale-free scalar suffices in dimension two --- the same reason, in a
different guise, that one reciprocal coordinate suffices in Theorem~\ref{cor:reciprocal}.

\subsection{Failure in higher dimension}\label{sec:obstruction}

\begin{proposition}[Non-identifiability for $n\ge3$]\label{prop:obstruction}
For $n=3$ the pair $(\tr\Sig,\det\Sig)$ does not determine the spectral shape: the positive
triples $(1,8,12)$ and $(2,3,16)$ have the same trace $21$ and the same determinant $96$, hence
the same value of every scale-free invariant built from trace and determinant alone, but
different extreme-eigenvalue ratios $12$ and $8$.
\end{proposition}

This too is background mathematics, not a new theorem. By Newton's identities a spectrum is
determined by all $n$ elementary symmetric functions, of which trace and determinant are the
complete list only when $n=2$; the failure above is the elementary consequence. The invariant
theory behind such statements is classical, and the statistical form is Mauchly's
\cite{Mauchly1940,Muirhead1982}.

We stress the relation to Section~\ref{sec:effdim}. Proposition~\ref{prop:obstruction} is about
the \emph{spectrum} of an $n\times n$ covariance; Proposition~\ref{prop:factor} is about the
\emph{support of the heterogeneity}. They are different statements, but they point the same way:
the one-coordinate closure used throughout this paper is a two-dimensional phenomenon. If the
excitation genuinely spans three or more modes with distinct rates, neither the palindromic
determinant nor the spectral closure is available, and we make no claim about that case.

\section{Relation to existing work, and what we claim}\label{sec:priorart}

\paragraph{Standard ingredients.} The following are classical and are used as such: the
stationary OU covariance and the Lyapunov equation \cite{Gardiner2004,Vatiwutipong2019,Simoncini2016};
the modal solution \eqref{eq:modal} for symmetric drift, standard in heterogeneous-OU work
\cite{Barucca2014,GodrecheLuck2019,FerreiraMetzBarucca2025}; the determinant as Wilks's
generalized variance \cite{Wilks1932} and its entropy reading \cite{CoverThomas2005}; graph
Laplacian eigenmodes and their use in network dynamics \cite{BamiehEtAl2012}; the reduction of a
self-reciprocal polynomial through $t+1/t$ \cite{Vieira2019}; the trace/determinant shape
statistic \cite{Mauchly1940,Muirhead1982,WolkowiczStyan1980,HornJohnson2012}.

\paragraph{Closest located prior art.} The nearest genuine antecedent is the work of Summers,
Cortesi and Lygeros on submodularity and controllability in complex networks
\cite{SummersEtAl2016}, which proves that $\log\det$ of the controllability Gramian is a monotone
increasing and submodular \emph{set} function of the chosen input columns (see also
\cite{Antoulas2005}). That is the qualitative predecessor of the present result, and it differs in
four ways that matter: it concerns a controllability Gramian rather than the stationary covariance
solving \eqref{eq:lyap}; it is a set function over discrete input subsets rather than a continuous
one-parameter family; the forcing is \emph{added} with no baseline constraint, whereas
\eqref{eq:forcing} reshapes a fixed isotropic baseline; and it yields no closed form, no
palindromic structure and no threshold. We are sharpening a qualitative monotonicity statement
into an exact identity in a narrower setting, not contradicting or subsuming it.

Two-temperature linear Langevin systems
\cite{GodrecheLuck2019,FilligerReimann2007,DotsenkoEtAl2013} share the model class exactly --- in
particular \cite[\S2.10]{GodrecheLuck2019} gives the explicit $2\times2$ stationary covariance for
arbitrary friction and diffusion --- but their scalar diagnostics are entropy production,
irreversibility and heat flux, not the covariance determinant. Likewise
\cite{BoffiDeGregorio2024} analyses precisely the two-degree-of-freedom two-temperature geometry
studied here and tracks an individual variance entry rather than the generalized variance. Low-rank
structure in Lyapunov solutions is well studied
\cite{Simoncini2016,TownsendWilber2018}, but for solution approximation and singular-value bounds
rather than determinants.

\paragraph{What we claim.} We are deliberately narrow. The determinant identity
\eqref{eq:sharp} is the $2\times2$ Hadamard expansion \eqref{eq:hadexp} applied to the
Cauchy matrix, i.e.\ the equality case of Oppenheim's inequality with the remainder written
out (Remark~\ref{rem:hadamard}); it is not claimed. The Cauchy--Hadamard form
\eqref{eq:hadamard} of a Sylvester solution is standard
\cite{PalittaLefteriu2022,YadykinEtAl2023}. The reciprocal coordinate is an invertible function
of the usual two-temperature combination (Remark~\ref{rem:wknown}) and is not claimed. The
reduction of a palindromic polynomial through $t+1/t$ is classical \cite{Vieira2019}. The
closest published use of the same pipeline we located is
\cite[\S4.5]{SchlickDemissie2011}, which forms a discrete-time steady-state covariance as a
Cauchy--Hadamard product and applies Oppenheim to bound its determinant, stopping at the
inequality.

\paragraph{Determinants of Lyapunov solutions.} There is a substantial control-theory
literature on the solution of the algebraic Lyapunov equation, and it is worth saying what shape
it has: it consists of \emph{bounds} --- on eigenvalues, on the trace, and on the determinant ---
rather than of exact expressions; see the survey \cite{KwonEtAl1996}. We did not locate an exact
determinant identity in that line. Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} is exact but, as
Remark~\ref{rem:hadamard} explains, it is exact only because $n=2$ makes the Hadamard expansion
terminate, so it does not compete with that literature.

\paragraph{Determinants of network covariances.} One line of work does use a covariance
determinant as a network performance measure: Siami and Motee's \emph{uncertainty volume} is a
log-determinant of the steady-state output covariance of a noisy consensus network, and sits
alongside the more familiar trace-type measures \cite{SiamiMotee2018}. It differs from the
present setting in every respect that matters here --- the noise is homogeneous, the drift is a
pure Laplacian, and the optimization is over which edges to add rather than over a scalar
coupling, with no threshold in a heterogeneity parameter. The network-coherence literature it
grows out of \cite{BamiehEtAl2012} is otherwise uniformly trace-type. Separately, ``generalized
variance'' in the Granger-causality and integrated-information literature
\cite{BarrettSeth2011} denotes the determinant of a \emph{residual} covariance, a different
matrix from $\Sig(\kappa)$; the two should not be conflated.

What we did not locate, in the literature we searched, is:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item the \emph{trace criterion} of Theorem~\ref{thm:trace} --- a condition $\tr H=q_b$
  controlling a palindromic symmetry of a covariance determinant in a deformation parameter;
\item any statement that a covariance or Gramian determinant is palindromic or self-reciprocal
  in a forcing or temperature parameter;
\item the resulting exact generalized-variance threshold, minimized over a coupling strength.
\end{enumerate}
We describe these as an \emph{apparently distinct assembly} and make no priority claim. Should
an equivalent result exist, the right reading of this paper is as an exact derivation and
machine-checked formalization of it. The search was English-language web and arXiv only, with no
access to MathSciNet, zbMATH or publisher full text, and no matrix-analysis textbook was
consulted in full; Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} in particular is exactly the sort of statement that
may appear as an exercise in a standard reference.

\paragraph{Relation to the author's other work.} The two-node model of \cite{DeJesus2026ou} is
recovered in Section~\ref{sec:K2} as the corollary $\tau=r^2$, $c^2=s^2=1/2$; that paper is the
motivating special case and is cited rather than reproduced. Separately, \cite{DeJesus2026dual}
records a reciprocal invariant $\tr(T)\tr(T^{-1})$ determining a two-dimensional shape ratio up
to inversion, and is relevant as conceptual background on one-coordinate completeness in two
dimensions. We state explicitly that the present theorem is derived independently from Lyapunov
covariance dynamics; \cite{DeJesus2026dual} is invariant-theoretic context, not a source for it.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
\item The results are exact statements about a linear-Gaussian model. No nonlinear, biological,
  cognitive, social or physical-universality claim is made, and no significance attaches to the
  numerical value of any constant beyond this model class.
\item \textbf{The drift is assumed symmetric} on the relevant invariant subspace, so that the
  modal basis is orthonormal and \eqref{eq:modal} holds. Non-normal drift is outside the scope
  of every theorem here.
\item Theorems~\ref{thm:sharp}--\ref{thm:trace} concern a $2\times2$ block, i.e.\ a
  configuration of distinct-rate dimension two (Section~\ref{sec:effdim}). With three distinct
  rates and genuine three-mode support the response is generically cubic
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:cubic}) and nothing here applies.
\item The palindromicity criterion $\tr H=q_b$ is an identity between polynomials. It carries no
  positivity: for indefinite $H$ the coefficient $K$ may be negative and $Q(\tau)$ may leave the
  positive-definite cone, in which case the formula is still true but is no longer a statement
  about a forcing covariance. The admissible family of Definition~\ref{def:admissible} is where
  the stochastic reading is valid.
\item The threshold theorem additionally requires $C>0$, i.e.\ $K>0$. The case $K=0$ is
  attainable (Proposition~\ref{prop:Ksign}) and has no threshold at all.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:factor} --- the $n$-dimensional reduction --- is proved analytically
  and is \emph{not} machine-checked; and it needs all five hypotheses of
  Remark~\ref{rem:factorhyp}, not merely that the forcing is supported in the active plane.
\item The formalization verifies algebra, not stochastic-process theory. That the $\Sig$ of
  \eqref{eq:lyap} is the stationary covariance of \eqref{eq:sde} is classical and assumed.
\item ``Distinct-rate dimension'' (Section~\ref{sec:effdim}) is interpretive vocabulary attached
  to a hypothesis, not an independent theorem.
\item We work out three graph corollaries. We claim no general characterization of which graphs
  and forced nodes satisfy the hypothesis, and no general bound on $w^{*}_{\min}$ over graphs.
\item Optimizing $w^{*}$ over $\kappa$ treats coupling as freely tunable and the forcing
  geometry as fixed; other optimizations give other constants.
\item The literature searches behind Section~\ref{sec:priorart} used web search, the arXiv API,
  Crossref and OpenAlex full-text search, together with a number of primary sources read in
  full. They did \emph{not} reach Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, MathSciNet or zbMATH,
  and arXiv's own search is metadata-only, so a result appearing as an unnamed mid-paper remark
  would be invisible to them. No matrix-analysis monograph was consulted in full; a
  Hadamard-product problems section is a plausible home for \eqref{eq:hadexp}. Two specific
  gaps are worth naming: the two-mode \emph{quantum} Gaussian literature, where
  $\det\sigma$ for two thermal modes versus coupling is computed routinely (purity being
  $1/\sqrt{\det\sigma}$), was not searched at all; and non-English literature was not searched.
  ``Not located'' throughout should therefore be read as the outcome of a documented but
  non-exhaustive sweep, not as a claim of absence.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Open items}\label{sec:open}

Recorded so that the gaps are explicit rather than silent.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
\item \textbf{Characterizing admissible configurations.} Which pairs (graph, forced node) give a
  node indicator with modal support in exactly two eigenmodes? The $P_3$ centre node works because
  of a symmetry-induced vanishing; $K_3$ works because of eigenvalue degeneracy. A general
  criterion is open, and is a question about graph symmetry rather than about the present theorem.
\item \textbf{Beyond rank one.} For a rank-$k$ deformation with $k\ge2$, or an excitation spanning
  three or more distinct-rate modes, the determinant is a polynomial of higher degree in the
  heterogeneity parameters and no single reciprocal coordinate is expected to suffice. We have not
  investigated whether a weaker structured reduction survives.
\item \textbf{Non-normal drift.} The modal formula \eqref{eq:modal} assumes a symmetric drift on
  the active plane. The non-normal case is open here.
\end{enumerate}
